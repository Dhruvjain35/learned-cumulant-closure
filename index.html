QUANTUM OPTICS × MACHINE LEARNING

The standard approximation misses this burst of light by 30%. The learned closure lands within 1.5% on 30 systems it never saw.

Put a few excited atoms in a mirrored cavity and they synchronize, dumping their energy in one bright flash called superradiance. Simulating it exactly costs exponentially more with every atom, so physicists truncate the equations. The standard truncation overshoots the flash by about 30%, and the next two orders don't fix it: one undershoots and the other blows up. This project trains a small, physics-constrained neural network to supply exactly the term the truncation throws away.

What happens with more atoms

The network was trained on systems of up to five atoms. Past that, the exact answer gets very expensive, so these checks use symmetry-reduced exact solves (no disorder) and quantum-trajectory simulations (with disorder). Its error grows about 0.8–0.9 points per atom, to 8–9% at sixteen. That's still about three times better than the standard method, but it's no longer the near-exact match it is at five.

How it works

The method keeps the cheap equations and learns only what they leave out.

01

Track the cheap moments

Follow averages and pair correlations of the cavity field and every atom. At five atoms that's 141 equations. The exact quantum state already has 512 dimensions, and it doubles with every atom you add.

02

Find what's missing

Those equations need three-body correlations. The standard closure sets their connected part (κ₃) to zero. Tracing the error shows it's two specific channels: photon number × inversion, and field × polarization × inversion.

03

Learn only that term

A shared 2×128 network predicts κ₃ from local moments, detunings and a few system-wide averages. It's built to respect atom-swap symmetry and Hermiticity, and a memory-aware branch handles the low-loss regime.

Why you can trust the numbers

Written down first
The 30-system test, its pass/fail bands and its random seeds were committed before any system ran. The models were frozen with SHA-256 hashes before final testing.
Reproduced outside
The baseline result (the 2nd-order overshoot) was re-run in a separate Julia codebase (QuantumCumulants.jl) by a researcher at Notre Dame's Center for Research Computing. The exact peaks matched to within 4×10⁻⁴, and the overshoot came out the same. The learned closure itself hasn't been run outside yet.
Negatives kept
Three hypotheses that didn't survive testing (a routing rule, an atom-count scaling story, a disorder story) are reported as failures, not deleted.
Limits measured
In the low-loss regime, long runs can drift outside physical bounds from about ten atoms, even when the burst peak is still right. It hasn't been tested on other starting states or other disorder shapes.
